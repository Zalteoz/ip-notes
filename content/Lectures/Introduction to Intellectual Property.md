# Current issues

## Patent Law

>[!def] Definition
>Protects new, functional inventions, processes, or machines for a limited time (typically 20 years)

>[!note] Purpose
>The purpose of patents is to have **more innovation**, a good example for this is the medical industry.

This allows companies to invest in labs that are making breakthroughs and guarantee the exclusivity of their product, thus making a return on investment.

But this can also be ***really bad***, for example in the COVID-19 pandemic, there were a lot of discussions about not allowing **patents** so that more people could get access to the vaccine.

The response from Pfizer was that the issue was not the patent but the manufacturing capability of the vaccines, so it was really a non issue.

**Licensing agreements** still happened. (In this case sublicenses, since giving licenses to hundreds of companies isn't what Pfizer has time to do. (see lecture slides *slide 14*))

>[!properties] Licensing agreements
>We allow another entity to use our invention, technology, and we're allowed to put additional conditions on that collaboration (money, percentage, etc).

## Copyright

>[!def] Definition
>Copyrights protect **original creative works**

These days, copyrights have become a little bit more complicated, for example with the Fortnite example. Some people tried to have a lawsuit with Epic Games because they have used their dance move in one emote.

But now we ask ourselves, what is *copyrightable*? 

For the case above, a good indicator is that a ***simple dance move is not eligible*** but a **complete choreography routine** is eligible. 

>[!example] The backpack kid
>This one was not accepted since it was only a single dance move and not a whole choreography.

## Trademark

>[!def] Definition
>Protects the **brand identity** elements like names, slogans, logos, and sounds that distinguish the service provided by the brand.

The idea is to build goodwill with the consumers so that they quickly find the products they seek. For example a Coke bottle's shape is uniquely recognizable, and the chocolate bunnies from Lindt are also really recognizable for any Swiss person.

The shape of the bunny being protected is really good for Lindt because it makes the consumer associate the product strongly with the brand.

The general idea is that **generic names cannot be trademarked**, for example Gruyère couldn't be trademarked, since it was also a name used internationally for a type of cheese, and not the Gruyère cheese factory itself.

It was also said in the lecture that you can't name your fruits shop after the name of a fruit for some reason, but it's fine for Apple to be a company making computers. (ASK)

>[!note] Purpose
>The whole goal is to associate strongly some products, some slogans, logos to a brand

### Running out of trademarks?

Nowadays, almost every single naturally occurring word has been **claimed**, so now we need to be really creative and create new words or make portmanteaus or other tricks. (Lyft, Flickr, Tumblr)

This means that the trademarks nowadays are of fairly **low quality** since they're not generic names.

This market trend is so strong (trademarking every word), that some celebrities now trademark their own name, children's name, in the hope of them having strong brand recognition in the future.

## Design

There are many cases where **design law** and **copyright law** overlap, and EU law provides something called **cumulation of rights** which enables something to benefit of both sides of the law in the right circumstances.

>[!note] EU law and Swiss law
>Generally, when we talk about EU law, we also include Swiss law inside of that. In IP law, **Switzerland follows EU law.**
