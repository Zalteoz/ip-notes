# Legal Requirements

Patents protect an **invention**, it's an instruction on how to solve a particular problem using technical means.

An invention is different than a **discovery**, an intention must be **repeatable**, think of the property of known material vs property put to use in invention

- @ Example:     (Material withstands mechanical shock vs. railway component making use of property)
- @ Example: (Gene discovered to exist in nature vs. use of gene in making polypeptide or in gene therapy)

## Novelty

Something is considered new when it's not part of the **state of the art**

>[!note] State of the art
>Everything made **available to the public** before the date of filing.

This means that the best thing to do is to keep your invention confidential until you have filed your application. Since otherwise, someone else could copy it and make it part of the **state of the art**, removing the novelty from your invention.

The first to file a patent application will be **entitled to the patent**

The requirements are the following:

- ? The disclosure can be **anywhere** in the world and in **any form** (even an Instagram post where the invention appears before the filing of the patent application, even then, you might be fucked and the invention is not novel anymore)
- ? Public disclosure before the filing of the patent application (anywhere in the world) ***will invalidate the patent***
- ? Novelty judged in relation to “prior art”
- ? Publically available
- ? Mere **hypothetic possibility of knowledge of the invention by experts sufficient**
- ? Typical question: Are all the components of the claim of the invention known? All it takes to destroy novelty is for a single prior art item to disclose all the features of the claimed invention.


>[!properties] Assessing novelty
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310102418.png)
>
>We look at what was in the state of the art before the filing of the application to check whether or not this is a novelty or not.

The general idea is that **when the public has used it before**, there is no novelty anymore. This is a very vague idea and depends on the cases, but it's a simple check to know if something is novel or not.

>[!note] When can we sue?
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310105357.png)

>
>If they refuse, once your patent gets granted, you can sue them, since they were aware that the patent application was in process (you sent them the application).

## Non-obviousness

>[!note] General idea
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310103710.png)

So for every invention, we look at it from the point of view of this person, for example in the example above, for someone who's worked with teapots their entire life, adding another spout **will not seem like an invention**. 

We try to make this fictional person a person that would be able to invent this product, with the education that someone who would invent it would have, with the experience of someone who could've invented that as well. 

For example if we're reviewing a vaccine, we would think of some **lab scientist** with some years of experience and some form of higher education.

It is important to note that this person **has no imagination**, but it can look at **prior art** and **assemble them together and tweak them** to some extent relative to their skill level.


>[!note] Definitions
>**Non obviousness**: an invention that an average “person skilled in the art” cannot only come up
>with by rational thinking. There is an “information gap” that the inventor solves in an intuitive,
>creative, associative way
>
>**Obviousness**:  an invention that for an average “person skilled in the art” is logical, when
systematically putting together “prior arts”

>[!properties] Indicators for both definitions
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310104206.png)

>[!properties] Assessing Non-obviousness
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310104224.png)
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310104238.png)
>
>Here, the fictional person looks at two previous inventions and combines them, and gets the same advantages as the new invention, thus we could say that this invention is **obvious** in some way.


We're trying to think the following: 
>"*if this fictional person were trying to fix the same problem that the new invention fixes, would they have been able to come up with the same solution by looking at prior arts and using their skill set and what they've learned in their education, but no imagination*".

## Disclosure

An invention needs to be **disclosed** for getting the patent. It must be *made available to the public*, we can't just file for a patent and keep the details secret to ourselves.

Note that this isn't an issue for the inventor, since getting the patent accepted would mean that their invention will be **protected** by the patent, so disclosure is not an issue.


>[!note] General idea
>![](Pasted%20image%2020260310104821.png)

The requirements are the following:

- ? **Sufficiency and clarity of the description**: A detailed description of at least one way of carrying out the invention must be given
- ? The information disclosed must be **sufficiently clear to a person “skilled in the art”** (cf. Art. 83 EPC) ("sufficiency“)
- ? The information disclosed is published in the patent document so that everyone can benefit from it
