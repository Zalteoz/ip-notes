# Trademarks

## Trademarks - Duration

**Trademarks** are the only ones out of the 4 that can actually last **indefinitely**, however, they are registered for only $10$ years, and after that, you need to renew them if you want to keep those.

>[!properties] Reminder
>If you don't use your trademark for $5$ years, it may no longer be enforced
>
>To consider it used, it is sufficient to:
>- License it and let the license be used
>- If the trademark is used for export purposes only
>- If the trademark is used in a different form, for example if Coca Cola stops making bottles, but still have other trademarked products, they won't lose their mark.


***
## Trademarks - Exhaustion

Trademarks also have exhaustion to some extent like **copyrights and patents** 
However, in **European** trademark law, there exists a **EEA-wide** exhaustion, but **international exhaustion doesn't exist**!

>[!note] Switzerland 
>In Switzerland, we only have **international exhaustion**, so you can resell anywhere in the world, but there are exceptions like pharma, etc.

The reason why we need exhaustion is that there are large price differences between trademarked products in different countries, for example with pharmaceuticals, because of state intervention in prices.

Sometimes there is also a need to **repackage** and **re-label** goods because of legal or cultural reasons.
This is why we need exhaustion for trademarks as well.

## Trademarks - Other limitations

>[!properties] Example application of articles
>![[Pasted image 20260519103521.png]]
>
>You're always allowed to **use the name of a mark if you're producing spare parts**, for example here the brand can say that their razor blades fit all *Gillette* shaves, as long as it's clear they're not officially affiliated with them.
>
>You **cannot prohibit someone to use their own name or use a geographic name** (if it characterizes the product, here the example with spring water makes sense) for a brand if it is already trademarked, you cannot prohibit *John Deere* from opening up a shop because he violates your *John Deere* trademark


### Comparative Advertising

Comparative advertising is allowed under certain conditions:
- It is not **misleading**
- **Objectively** compares similar goods or services
- Does not take **unfair advantage** of the reputation of a trademark
- Does not present goods or services as **imitations** or replicas of trademarked goods or services

# Design Rights

## Design Rights - Duration

An **EU Registered Community Design** and a **Swiss Design** protection lasts $5$ years, and you can renew it for up to a total term of $25$ years.

## Design Rights - Other limitations

>[!properties] Example application of articles
>![[Pasted image 20260519105004.png]]
>
>**Paragraph 1**:
>If a product looks a certain way only because it **has to look that way** to work, you cannot protect the design right
>
>**Paragraph 2**:
>This denies design protection to the exact connection joints, points, dimensions needed to make two products fit together mechanically. Apple cannot have a design right on the USB-C port that prohibits others from making iPhone accessories.
>
>**Paragraph 3**:
>Lego exception to paragraph 2, if the connection is part of a **modular, creative building system**, they **can be protected by design law**.

#### Other limitations

- **Private** and non-commercial purposes (you're free to make copies of designs privately)
- **Experimental purposes** (researchers can replicate a design to study and test it, innovate upon it)
- **Reproducing** for the purposes of citation and **teaching** (You are legally allowed to use images or illustrations of a protected design in textbooks, but you need to acknowledge the source/owner)
- **Exhaustion** (They cannot prevent you from reselling your Gucci glasses on eBay). Note that this depends on what kind of exhaustion applies.
