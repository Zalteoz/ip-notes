# Copyrights

## Rights conferred by Copyrights

>[!note] U.K Copyright Act
>![[Pasted image 20260428111402.png]]

The owner has the **exclusive right** to perform or authorize several acts:

>[!def] Acts in question
>- **Reproduction** of the work 
>- **Issuing copies**, renting or lending the work to the public
>- **Performing**, showing or playing the work in public
>- **Communicating** the work to the public (including via the Internet)
>- Making an **adaptation** or derivative work

***

### Types of Rights

Copyright is generally divided into **three** categories:
1. **Economic Rights**: These allow the creator to derive financial reward from the use of their works by others (reproduction, distribution, rental, etc.)
2. **Moral Rights**: These protect the non-economic interests of the author
	- Right to **claim authorship**
	- Right to **object to distortions**, mutilations or modifications (integrity)
	- Right of **publication**

>[!danger] Note
>In many European countries, these moral rights cannot be **waived nor transferred**, which is a problem namely with **ghostwriters** for example

3. **Neighboring Rights**: Rights granted to people who are not the *author* but help make the work available (performers, film producers, broadcasters).
	- In Europe these rights are separate from the Copyright, but in the U.S those are granted as part of Copyright

***

## How infringement is determined

 Infringement occurs when a substantial part of the work is used without permission.  

- **Originality & Causal Connection:** There must be a link between the original and the copy. If two people independently create the exact same thing without seeing each other's work, it is **not** infringement.  
- **Subconscious Copying:** This can still be considered an infringement.  
- **Literal vs. Non-literal:** Copying doesn't have to be exact; copying the structure or specific protected expression also counts.

>[!note] Infringement Types
>- **Primary Infringement:** Direct acts like copying or distributing.  
>- **Secondary Infringement:** Dealing with infringing copies or operating a network (like P2P) that facilitates infringement.

***

## Copyright - Remedies

 The types of remedies available are very similar to those in patent law and depend on national legislation.  

- ? **Interim or preliminary injunctions** (Immediate stop).  
- ? **Final or permanent injunctions** (Permanent stop).  
- ? **Destruction or surrender** of infringing products.  
- ? **Recovery of damages**.

***

### Enforcement Tools

 Because monitoring every single use of a work is impossible for one person, we use:  

- **Collecting Societies:** (e.g., **SUISA** for music, **ProLitteris** for literature and visual arts, **SSA** for dramatic works, ... in Switzerland). They act as a "one-stop shop" for licenses and enforcement.  
- **Criminal Law:** In severe cases.  
- **Customs/Border Control:** To stop physical infringing goods.

Collecting societies are especially important in this day and age, it would be impossible for an artist to catch **every infringement**, it's much easier for a *centralized force* to crack down on infringements, and also easier to do business with people who would like to acquire licenses from multiple copyrights.

***

## Copyright - Licenses and Transfers

 Licensing is how we give others permission to use our work. This is governed by **national contract laws**.

 >[!danger] Transferability  (Reminder)
>
>- **Economic Rights:** These **can** be licensed or transferred.  
>- **Moral Rights:** In many jurisdictions, these **always stay with the author** and cannot be sold.

***

### The Internet Challenge

 Enforcing copyright on the internet presents unique difficulties because content uploaded in one country is accessible worldwide.  
 
>[!properties] Enforcement Steps  
>If your work is disseminated without consent, you can take the following actions:  
>
>- @ Send a **cease and desist letter** to the infringer.  
>- @ Request the **ISP (Internet Service Provider)** to disable access to or remove the infringing content.  
>- @ Consider legal action against the end user.  
>- **Note on Liability:** Some ISPs are legally exempted from liability for the content their users post.  

To protect works from future infringement, creators often apply **technical protection measures** or adapt their broader business strategies.

# Trademarks

## Rights conferred by Trademarks

 Registration of an EU trademark confers **exclusive rights** on the proprietor. This allows the owner to prevent third parties from using identical or similar signs in the course of trade without authorization.


>[!note] EU community Trademark Regulation
>![[Pasted image 20260428115627.png]]
>![[Pasted image 20260428115633.png]]

 The owner can **prohibit use** under three main conditions:  

1. **Double Identity:** The third party uses an identical sign for identical goods or services.  
2. **Likelihood of Confusion:** The sign is identical or similar to the trademark and used for identical or similar goods, creating a risk that the public confuses the two or associates them.  
3. **Famous Marks:** If the trademark has a **reputation**, the owner can prevent its use even for non-similar goods if that use is detrimental to the mark's character (tarnishing).

>[!properties] Clarification on Famous Marks
> For a **famous mark**, the owner can prevent its use on **any product** regardless of whether there is a "likelihood of confusion". The law protects the **value and reputation** of the brand itself, not just the consumer from being confused.
> 
> This means that I cannot start selling apples under the company name **Gucci**, even though it has nothing to do with luxury, this would tarnish the **luxury and prestige** of the brand, and would also give me an unfair advantage by abusing of the **Gucci** name to sell my apples

***

### Infringing Acts

 Prohibited activities specifically include:  

- @ Affixing the sign to goods or packaging.  
- @ Offering, marketing, or stocking goods under the sign.  
- @ Importing or exporting goods under the sign.  
- @ Using the sign as a company name or in advertising.  
- **Customs Enforcement:** Authorities can prevent goods from third countries from entering the country if they bear an unauthorized identical trademark.

***

### Technicalities of Trademark Protection

 A trademark must be used "**as a mark**" for an infringement to occur; this means it is used to distinguish one's own products from a competitor's.

>[!properties] Principle of Specialty
> The scope of protection is generally limited to the specific **classification** of goods and services for which the mark was registered.
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260428120659.png]]
> 
> The objects above all use the mark **Lotus**, but there's no problem here because of this principle.

>[!danger] Exception
>"Famous" marks (like Coca-Cola or Gucci) receive broader protection that can extend beyond their specific product category, as discussed above with the Gucci apples example. This is done to prevent three things:
>
>1. **Blurring**: detriment to the distinctiveness of the mark
>2. **Dilution by tarnishing or detriment** to the repute of the trade mark
>3. **Free-riding**: taking unfair advantage of the distinctiveness of the mark

# Registered Design Rights

## Right conferred by Design Rights

 A registered design protects the aesthetic appearance of a product. The owner has the exclusive right to prohibit others from commercially exploiting the design.

>[!note] Scope of protection
> Protection covers any design that does **not** produce a **different overall impression** on an "informed user".

>[!example] Example from the lecture with sauna
>We saw an example in the lecture about **sauna parts**, namely, some design had been protected where there were *metal bars going through the wooden parts* of the sauna parts.
>
>Another company had been designated as an *infringer* because their sauna designs also included wooden parts being pierced by metal rods.
>
>The court decided that the overall **idea** of the original sauna parts was that whole idea of metal rods going through wooden parts, therefore they decided that this was indeed an infringement.

 The owner can prevent the following acts regarding a product incorporating the design:  
 
- ? Making  
- ? Offering for sale  
- ? Putting on the market  
- ? Importing/Exporting  
- ? Stocking  

When assessing the scope of protection, courts must take into account the degree of freedom the designer had when developing the design.
