# Patents

## Patents - Duration

Patent protection may last up to **20 years**, but it may last shorter if:
- The *renewal fees* are not paid
- If the patent is **revoked or withdrawn**

>[!note] Note
>The patent term is counted from the **filing date** of the patent application
>Thus it is automatically shorter than **20 years**

### Patent protection extension

Since **pharma and plant protection products** take a lot longer to approve, as a compensation, *Supplementary protection certificates* can last up to $5$ to $5.5$ years.

>[!tip] SPC's
>SPC are granted by **national patent offices** since they relate to national patents.
>-> Limited territorial scope

## Patents - Exhaustion

>[!def] Swiss Patent Act
>![[Pasted image 20260512102550.png]]

The Exhaustion Principle, also called the **First Sale Principle** in the US it states that once a specific IP-protected product is **sold legally** and without restrictions by the rights holder, their control over that specific item is **exhausted**

- The buyer is **free to resell**, lend, or give away that specific product without needing further permission or paying additional royalties to the original creator

>[!danger] Limit
>You can sell the physical item you bought, but you **cannot make copies** of it 


The law varies significantly depending on where the *first sale* occurs:
#### National Exhaustion 

Rights are only exhausted if the first sale happens **within that specific country**

>[!properties] Switzerland example
>In Switzerland, for certain sectors like patented medicine, the rights holder can often block "parallel imports" (buying the drug cheaper abroad and bringing it into Switzerland) because the sale abroad didn't "*exhaust*" the Swiss patent right.

***
#### Regional Exhaustion

Rights are exhausted across a **group of countries**

>[!example] Example
>In the European Economic Area (EEA), a sale in Germany exhausts the rights in France. This prevents companies from segmenting the European market.

***

#### International Exhaustion

Rights are exhausted **anywhere in the world

>[!note] Meaning
>If you buy a product legally in Tokyo, the IP owner cannot stop you from **importing and reselling** it in a country that follows international exhaustion.

# Copyrights

## Copyrights - Duration

Before the publication of the **EU Term Directive** in $1993$, the copyright term was not harmonized, but now, the term is set at **70 years post mortem auctoris**, meaning 70 years after the death of the original author.

The US also adopted this term in $1998$

>[!tip] Special case
>In 2011, a EU Directive prolonged the term of protection for **performers and phonogram producers** from 50 years to **70** years after the *first performance/fixation*

>[!note] Swiss Law
>
>There is a slight difference for **computer programs**
>
>![[Pasted image 20260512104113.png]]

There is evidence to suggest that the reason why we keep making the copyright term **longer** is because of **lobbyism**, huge companies like **Disney** want to keep their copyrights as long as possible and apply a lot of pressure.

## Copyrights - Private copy

>[!attention] Note
>Most of the material in this section are illustrations, which are also found below for convenience.

### Open ended vs Closed list limitation

The European Union has a **closed list**, meaning that you get a list of $25$ *scenarios* in which you're fine and are considered doing **private copy**, and if your use case doesn't fit within this list, you're **infringing the copyright**

>[!example] Examples from the list
>![[Pasted image 20260512105218.png]]

In the **US** we do something called **fair use defense**, we do a **test** based on four factors, and once we've looked at those, the judge decides if the use of the **copyrighted work** is **fair** or not.
Those are:

1. Purpose and character of use (commercial or non-profit educational)
	- Is it for **profit**, or is it "*transformative*" (e.g., a parody or commentary)?
2. Nature of the work
	- Is the original work **factual** (more likely to be fair use) or highly **creative** (less likely)?
3. Amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole
	- Did you use the whole thing or just a **small snippet**? Did you take the "heart" of the work? 
4. The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work
	- Does your use hurt the original creator's ability to **make money from their work**?

## Copyrights - Other limitations

The typical limitations (meaning it's ok to use them) are the following:
- **Transient/Incidental Reproductions:** 
	- This makes it legal for your computer to function. When you browse the web, your computer "copies" data into its RAM or cache. Without this limitation, just opening a website could technically be considered illegal copyright infringement.
- **Teaching/Research:** 
	- This allows a professor to show a diagram in a lecture or a researcher to quote a study without being sued.
- **Criticism/Parody:** 
	- This is what allows shows like _Saturday Night Live_ or YouTube reviewers to use clips of movies to make fun of them or critique them.
- **Public Places (Freedom of Panorama):** 
	- In many European countries, this makes it legal for you to take a photo of a famous building or a statue in a public square and share it, even though the architect or sculptor technically owns the copyright to that design.
- **Press reporting**

There are also some limitations specific to **particular work categories** for example you are allowed to reverse engineer a computer program.

>[!note] Unclear point
>You might have a contract with the software provider in the above case that asks you to promise not to reverse engineer the software. (Or other similar example)
>
>The question whether the **contract** or **copyright law** is stronger is **difficult to answer**, it's highly case specific and difficult to give a clear answer.
